# Build the equipment library around rates, not models

IntelCost's equipment library should be a list of **size classes**, not makes and models. Each row is a type plus a size band, defined by the spec estimators actually size by: operating weight for excavators, net HP for dozers, bucket yd³ for loaders, ROC for skid steers and CTLs, payload for trucks, and platform height for lifts. Each row carries a median bare rental day/week/4-week rate, an owned hourly rate with fuel removed, fuel burn in gal/hr at low/medium/high load, a transport class that drives mob/demob, and, for production equipment, one production record per activity. No public "national average" rental table exists. The defensible default is the **median of 2025–2026 dealer rate sheets**, sampled across six states and checked against national marketplace guides. The defensible owned default is **FEMA's 2025 schedule with Caterpillar telematics fuel backed out**. Both carry gaps the app has to disclose: there are no Texas, California or Florida sheets, many heavy classes have only one or two sources, and FEMA's rates are reimbursement ceilings rather than competitive owned costs. Production data is strong on method and weak on machine-specific numbers. Public US sources (Army FM 5-434, Peurifoy course notes, Cat articles, and DOT daily production tables) give reliable formulas, correction factors and crew-day sanity bounds. The licensed Cat Performance Handbook curves, such as dozer LCY/hr against push distance, are not public. On location, the evidence supports the split already decided. Equipment cost varies about one-third as much as labor across cities. Diesel varies on its own regional pattern (−9% to +31% against the US average this week). The three parts therefore need three separate adjustments. No incumbent combines what IntelCost's existing shared-equipment design already implies: picking the cheapest rental period, pooling one machine across activities, and treating mob/demob as a first-class cost. That combination is the opening.

## The design basis IntelCost has already set

This report treats the following as fixed decisions, not findings to be tested. Every equipment row carries both bare rental rates and an owned hourly rate. The operator is always excluded from the equipment rate and priced from labor at the project's saved wage set. Fuel is separate, calculated as gal/hr times a fuel price the user enters. Location is split three ways: a small equipment location factor on the equipment's own rate, a local fuel price, and location-adjusted wages from the Wage Calculator for the operator. Production rates exist only for production equipment: earthwork in depth, plus paving, compaction, trenching, pile driving, concrete pumping and utilities. Lifts, cranes, telehandlers and support equipment are priced by duration.

The cost-component design these rows feed is already built. Equipment components are lump or unit. Shared equipment resources live per division, with mob/demob counted once and spread by usage hours. Usage hours come from crew hours on a labor component or from a quantity formula. Labor components define crews. Assemblies copy components onto items and link shared equipment by name. The task here is to fill that structure with defensible data and a picker that fits it. The companion catalog, *IntelCost Equipment Catalog.xlsx*, was built from the same notes and holds the row-level values.

## Incumbents normalize everything to hourly and leave three jobs undone

The three heavy-civil leaders model equipment the same way. **Heavy-civil estimating product A** keeps a coded equipment library. Each record has an Inside (owned) or Outside (rented) type, an optional linked operator labor code, a **Rent Rate** and an **EOE Rate** covering "fuel, oil, maintenance and other costs", with HR as "the normal unit used for all equipment" (vendor documentation (link withheld, rule 9)). Equipment reaches an activity through a crew. Crew hours come from the production rate and are editable in both directions. Rows added outside the crew "are not updated when crew hours/productivity change" (vendor documentation (link withheld, rule 9)). **Heavy-civil estimating product B** lets a resource be costed per HR, DY, WK or MO. Fuel is computed as consumption × a global fuel rate. A Rental flag sends cost to a separate "Equipment Rented" bucket with its own markup. Product B needs "conversion factors" when equipment "is costed monthly but used daily" and allows only one associated labor type per machine (vendor documentation (link withheld, rule 9)). **Heavy-civil estimating product C** goes further and requires equipment rates to be hourly. It converts weekly and monthly amounts through an Hours Per Period helper (vendor documentation (link withheld, rule 9)), and it documents geographic area only for labor, not equipment.

Commercial tools are thinner. **A cloud takeoff product (D)** treats Equipment as one of four cost types, with a unit cost that is "typically your purchase price" and no time-based fields (vendor documentation (link withheld, rule 9)). **A construction-management platform (E)** maps an Equipment catalog item to cost type E, and no time-based pricing is documented (vendor documentation (link withheld, rule 9)). **A commercial estimating product (F)** prices labor and equipment through crews and rate tables. Through a cost-data publisher's crews, crew equipment cost per day is (hourly operating × 8) + (weekly rent ÷ 5), divided by daily output ([cost-data handbook excerpt](https://catalogimages.wiley.com/images/db/pdf/9781118025284.excerpt.pdf)). The richest owned-rate builder found is **the equipment cost calculator in a contractor ERP's estimating module (G)**. It takes delivered price, salvage, life, hours per year, finance and insurance percentages, gal/hr × fuel price by type, and service intervals. It has no operator, mob or rental-minimum fields (vendor documentation (link withheld, rule 9)). **A contractor project-management product (H)** stands out because it stores Idle Time and Down Time rates as estimate rates, alongside daily, weekly and monthly rental (vendor documentation (link withheld, rule 9)).

On rate data, **a rate-data publisher's rental rate book** is the reference standard. It publishes a monthly ownership rate, with daily, weekly and hourly rates derived "with increases to account for lost availability on shorter use periods". It publishes a separate hourly operating cost that excludes the operator. Its location and age adjustments apply to ownership only (vendor documentation (link withheld, rule 9)). The rate-data publisher sells cost and retail rental APIs covering more than 20,000 models, with retail rates updated quarterly (vendor documentation (link withheld, rule 9)). Its integration page names no estimating vendors (vendor documentation (link withheld, rule 9)), and licensing cost is unknown. A rental benchmarking service serves "equipment rental companies and dealers", not estimators (vendor documentation (link withheld, rule 9)).

Three jobs go undone across this set. None of the retrieved vendor documentation shows the app picking the cheapest rental period for a computed duration. Instead, incumbents convert everything to hourly or apply static conversion factors, which conflicts with how rentals are actually billed. None documents a project equipment pool, where one machine sits on site for a calendar span and activities draw hours from it, leaving idle time visible. None documents a dedicated mob/demob feature. IntelCost's shared-equipment resource, which counts mob once and spreads it by usage, already solves the second and third. The picker and rate engine should finish the first. One caution: several of product A's help articles (rent breakdown, EOE factors) did not render, so product A's handling of standby and mob is unconfirmed, not proven absent.

## Rental medians come from a Midwest-leaning dealer sample

There is no public US rental table by class, so the national average has to be built. The research averaged 2025–2026 published bare-rate sheets from dealers in Utah (Wheeler, a Cat Rental Store), Ohio (Columbus Equipment), Illinois (Burris), Minnesota (RTL), Missouri (Nu-Way) and Massachusetts (Ahearn). Each class was cross-checked against DOZR's March 2026 marketplace ranges. Each source counts once per class. Representative medians, as day / week / 4-week: **3–4 t mini excavator about $325 / $1,012 / $2,788**; **20–25 t excavator about $1,040 / $2,927 / $7,950**; **D6-class dozer about $2,111 / $5,730 / $15,656**; **3–3.2 yd wheel loader about $825 / $2,050 / $5,500**; **40–45 t ADT about $2,750 / $7,500 / $17,000** ([Wheeler 2025](https://wheelercat.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/2025-Construction-Equipment-Rental-Rates.pdf); [Columbus 2025](https://www.columbusequipment.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/CER-Rental-Rate-Sheet-2025_REVISED-6.25.pdf); [Burris 2025](https://burris-media-cloud.s3.us-west-2.amazonaws.com/2025/03/Rental-Rate-Guide-Printable.pdf); [RTL 2025](https://www.rtlequipment.com/fckimages/rtlequipment-RentalRateSheet-2025.pdf); [Nu-Way 2025](https://nuwayinc.com/Uploaded/Catalog/PDF/rentalrates_January2025.pdf); [Ahearn](https://ahearnrents.com/rates/); [DOZR](https://dozr.com/blog/excavator-rental-cost)).

The library should default to the **median, not the mean**. The Cat dealer runs 1.5–2× the independents on minis, rollers and CTLs: its 3–4 t mini is $528/day against a $325 median. A five- to seven-source mean is pulled visibly upward by one high outlier. Reliability also falls off steeply as machines get bigger. Minis, skid steers, CTLs, compaction, telehandlers and small support gear have four to seven sources each. D4–D8 dozers, 65 t+ excavators, graders, pavers and cold planers rest on one or two, mostly the Cat dealer. The sample has no Texas, California or Florida earthwork sheets because the national renters (United Rentals, Sunbelt, Herc, Sunstate) put prices behind login or ZIP selection ([URI example](https://www.unitedrentals.com/marketplace/equipment/material-handling/cranes/carry-deck-crane-15-tons)). The honest label is "national mid-market proxy", and each row should carry its source count and min–max range so the picker can show confidence. Scrapers and bare dump trucks have **no published rental rate at all**. Dump trucks trade operated: Minnesota's prevailing-wage minimum truck rental is **$104.77/hr for a 3-axle including driver** from May 2026 ([MN DLI](https://www.dli.mn.gov/business/employment-practices/prevailing-wage-region-1-minimum-truck-rental-rates)).

Aerial lifts are a weaker dataset. The most granular list is Sunbelt's Pennsylvania COSTARS not-to-exceed contract (March 2023–March 2024). It covers every class from 10 ft slab scissors to 185 ft straight booms: for example, a 19 ft electric scissor at $174 / $341 / $500 and a 60 ft articulating boom with jib at $601 / $1,390 / $2,632 ([PA COSTARS / Sunbelt](https://www.dgs.internet.state.pa.us/COSTARSElecBiddAdmin/Supplier/DownloadCustomList/4586)). Cross-checking against BigRentz's 23-city means (December 2022 data) and 2026 ranges from DOZR and ScaffoldExchange puts a 19 ft slab at about **$600–650 per 4 weeks** and a 45 ft articulating boom at about **$2,270** ([BigRentz](https://www.bigrentz.com/blog/scissor-lift-rental-cost); [DOZR](https://dozr.com/blog/scissor-lift-rental-cost); [ScaffoldExchange](https://scaffoldexchange.com/lifts-and-access/aerial-lift-rental-cost)). These are older than the earthwork data, and US rental rates have risen since 2022 by an amount no available index measures. Lift rows should be flagged as lower confidence until a 2026 renter sheet is obtained.

Rental terms set the billing logic. The industry standard is **one shift = 8 hr/day, 40 hr/week, 160 hr per 4 weeks**. Double shift bills 1.5× and triple shift 2× ([United Rentals terms](https://www.unitedrentals.com/legal/rental-service-terms-us)). The Cat dealer instead uses 176 hours per 4 weeks, which buys about 10% more machine time per period. Dealer ladders cluster at **week ≈ 3.0× day and 4-week ≈ 2.9× week**. Columbus prices explicitly at 2.5× / 2.5× ([Columbus 2025](https://www.columbusequipment.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/CER-Rental-Rate-Sheet-2025_REVISED-6.25.pdf)). Lifts run flatter, with week at about 2.2–2.8× day. Those ladders are why period choice matters. Four days of a 3–4 t mini at the median day rate costs $1,300, while one week costs $1,012. Add-ons sit on top: damage waiver at 10–16% of rent (16% at the Cat dealer, 10–15% per DOZR), an environmental fee of 2% capped at $99, and refuelling at the yard's posted price, $8/gal at Columbus ([Wheeler 2025](https://wheelercat.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/2025-Construction-Equipment-Rental-Rates.pdf); [DOZR](https://dozr.com/blog/excavator-rental-cost)).

Cranes and concrete pumps break the bare-rate model, because the market sells them operated. Published 2025–2026 operated crane rates run about **$210/hr for 20–25 t, $245 for 40 t, $330 for 75 t and $408 for 100–120 t**. They carry 3–8 hour minimums, portal-to-portal travel and 7–10% fuel surcharges ([North Texas Crane](https://www.northtexascrane.com/siteart/pdf/2025-Crane-Rental-Rate-Sheet-1.pdf); [Dependable Crane](https://dependablecrane.com/pricing/); [Central Florida Crane](https://centralfloridacraneservice.com/feeds/blog/crane-rental-rates-operator)). Boom pumps run about **$185/hr (38 m) to $225/hr (46 m) plus $4.00/yd³** in Ohio, against a national $200–350+/hr ([Concrete Pumping Ohio](https://concretepumpingohio.com/rental-rates/); [CCE News](https://cceonlinenews.com/concrete/concrete-pump-rental-cost-usa/)). Bare rough-terrain crane rentals do exist: Columbus lists a 50 t at $7,500 per 4 weeks. The library can therefore store a bare rate where one is published. It also needs an explicit "operated service" rate type for these classes, so the operator is not counted twice. Product B's documentation warns about exactly that double count (vendor documentation (link withheld, rule 9)).

## FEMA gives owned rates; Cat telematics strips the fuel

Three public schedules publish owned hourly rates, and all three include fuel. **FEMA's 2025 Schedule of Equipment Rates** applies to declarations on or after July 1, 2025, and no 2026 edition exists. Each rate covers "depreciation, overhead, all maintenance, field repairs, fuel, lubricants, tires" and excludes the operator ([FEMA](https://www.fema.gov/sr/node/500264)). It gives one all-in figure, with no ownership/operating split and no disclosed diesel price. **Caltrans' 2026–27 book** is also owned, fuel-included and operator-excluded. It was reissued in September 2026 because fuel rose ([Caltrans interim](https://dot.ca.gov/-/media/dot-media/programs/construction/documents/equipment-rental-rates-and-labor-surcharge/book_26_27_intv2.pdf)). Its dollar columns could not be extracted reliably. **USACE EP 1110-1-8** builds its rate bottom-up: ownership is depreciation plus cost of facilities capital, operating is fuel, FOG, repairs and tires, and standby is 50% of depreciation plus FCCM, capped at 40 hours a week. Its regional dollar tables could not be retrieved ([EP 1110-1-8](https://www.publications.usace.army.mil/Portals/76/Users/182/86/2486/EP%201110-1-8_RV1-12.pdf)).

FEMA is therefore the only usable dollar source. Getting a fuel-excluded owned rate means subtracting gal/hr × the diesel price embedded in the rate year. Caterpillar's December 2022 fuel tables come from VisionLink telematics and give low, medium and high burn defined by idle share: 50%, 30% and 10% ([Cat fuel tables](https://wheelercat.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/Cat-Performance-Handbook-from-VST-fuel-consumption-2022-12-09T21-20-09.pdf)). Using the EIA 2025 diesel average of $3.66 ([EIA](https://www.eia.gov/dnav/pet/hist/LeafHandler.ashx?n=PET&s=EMD_EPD2D_PTE_NUS_DPG&f=A)), a D6 works out to 135.15 − (4.82 × 3.66) = **$117.51/hr ex-fuel**, and a 320 GC to **$97.76**. Fuel was only 5–16% of FEMA's rate for most earthwork iron. Changing the diesel year moves the result by under 1%, while changing the gal/hr assumption moves it far more. Classes without a Cat table (trucks, compressors, generators, pumps) can fall back on the USACE fuel factor. It implies **0.0486 gal per bhp-hr** for diesel at full load, which against the D6's measured burn implies a load factor of about 46%.

Fuel is no longer a small share at today's prices, which makes the user-entered fuel price necessary. On-highway diesel averaged **$6.276/gal** in the week of October 19, 2026 ([EIA](https://www.eia.gov/petroleum/gasdiesel/)). At that price the D6's medium burn costs about $30/hr, roughly 20% of its ex-fuel + fuel total rather than the 13% embedded in FEMA's 2025 figure. Off-road dyed diesel excludes road taxes, so jobsite prices run below EIA's on-highway series. The default should come from the regional EIA figure, labeled as on-highway, with the user's own price taking precedence.

FEMA's numbers carry two reliability problems the app must surface. The first is extraction quality. Several rows look internally inconsistent: pickup rates do not rise with size, 17–45 kW generators are not monotonic, a 12 in diesel pump is listed at $4.75/hr, and a 13 CY mixer truck sits at three times the 11 CY rate. Water trucks, diesel trash pumps, welders and the Cat 450 backhoe did not extract at all ([FEMA 2025 PDF](https://www.fema.gov/sites/default/files/documents/fema_pa_schedule-equipment-rates_2025.pdf)). Every one needs manual verification against the PDF before loading. The second is positioning: FEMA rates are reimbursement rates, not bid-competitive costs. Compare FEMA ex-fuel against the median 4-week rental spread over 160 hours (this report's own arithmetic). The D6 comes in at 1.2×, 20–36 t excavators at about 2×, a 3 yd loader at 3.2× and a Cat 420 backhoe at 4.5×. A contractor would not bid an owned backhoe at four times the long-term rental cost per hour. The owned column should therefore be labeled as a conservative ceiling. The picker should show both columns side by side, and the company list should let a firm replace FEMA with its own internal rate. Off-highway and articulated trucks, scissor lifts and telescopic booms have **no FEMA row**. Their owned rates are either proxies (for lifts, the nearest articulating boom by height) or blanks the user fills in.

Standby and overtime need defaults because pooled equipment sits idle between activities. For a rental, idle days simply keep billing at the period rate. For owned equipment, the published rules differ. USACE sets standby at 50% of depreciation plus FCCM. Caltrans uses class-specific Delay Factors of about **0.09–0.22** of the rate for most classes (0.21 for aerial lifts, 0.22 for forklifts, 0.45 for pile leads), with overtime factors of about 0.80–0.92 ([Caltrans 2026–27](https://dot.ca.gov/-/media/dot-media/programs/construction/documents/equipment-rental-rates-and-labor-surcharge/book_26_27.pdf)). FEMA pays no standby except during intermittent use ([FEMA IS-1000](https://emilms.fema.gov/is_1000/groups/247.html)). Since only an all-in rate exists, a Caltrans-style delay factor per class is the workable default for owned idle time.

## Production data is a formula engine with DOT guardrails

Every earthwork activity reduces to one equation: volume per cycle × fill factor × cycles per hour × job efficiency × condition factors. The result is converted between bank, loose and compacted yards. Handbook values assume 100% efficiency: Caterpillar says its data "is based upon 100% efficiency in operation" and must be corrected ([Cat PHB Ed. 50](https://www.ringpower.com/media/2086/caterpillarperfhandbook_ed50.pdf)). The standard correction is FM 5-434's "50 minutes of production time per hour", or E = 0.83 ([FM 5-434](https://vulcanhammer.net/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/fm5_434.pdf)). The pieces that are public and reliable are:

- The soil conversion matrix from FM 5-434 Table 1-1, which checks out against three worked examples. Loam goes bank→loose 1.25, bank→compacted 0.90. Clay goes 1.43 and 0.90. Blasted rock goes 1.50 and 1.30.
- Dozer correction factors: operator 1.00 / 0.75 / 0.60 by day, material 0.60–1.20, slot dozing 1.20, and a weight factor of 2,300 ÷ lb/LCY. These match the factors used in Cat-curve problems.
- The excavator equation C × S × V × B × E, with FM cycle times of 14–15 s at optimum depth and Cat bucket fill factors ([Cat](https://www.cat.com/en_US/articles/ci-articles/bucket-fill-factors.html)).
- A wheel loader basic cycle of 27–33 s ([Cat](https://www.cat.com/en_US/articles/for-owners/how-to-calculate-cat-wheel-loader-productivity.html)).
- Truck balancing, N = truck cycle ÷ load time, with rolling resistance from 40 lb/ton on hard roads to 400 lb/ton in mud.
- Compaction, CCY/hr = 16.3 × W × S × L × E ÷ P ([KSU](https://fac.ksu.edu.sa/sites/default/files/Tutorial%207.pdf)).
- Grader area, ft²/hr = S × (Le − Lo) × 5,280 × E ([Cat](https://cat.com/en_US/articles/for-owners/measure-your-motor-grader-productivity.html)).

The gap is the machine-specific curves. Cat's dozer production curves (LCY/hr at 50–300 ft push for D3–D11 with S/SU/U blades) are not public. Neither are the excavator cycle-time charts, the scraper cycle-versus-haul charts or the rippability thresholds. The public Ed. 50 mirrors returned only front matter. These curves are the most valuable missing data in the whole library. Getting them means licensing (Cat or Komatsu handbook), or substituting Peurifoy's first-principles blade-load model, which needs blade dimensions and speeds. No credible public rate exists for hydraulic breakers, rock drills or chain trenchers. Those activities should launch as user-entered rates.

DOT production tables add a different layer: measured crew-day outcomes that already include trucking, traffic and setup delays. WisDOT reports medians with the top and bottom quartiles dropped. Its excavation rates are 600 CY/day by truck, 4,500 by scraper and 3,000 by articulated truck. Its HMA median is 1,300 TON/day ([WisDOT](https://www.wisconsindot.gov/Documents/doing-bus/eng-consultants/cnslt-rsrces/tools/estimating/production-rate-table.pdf)). TxDOT gives low/medium/high values: embankment 508 / 2,030 / 4,240 CY and RCP 80 / 119 / 212 LF/day ([TxDOT 2024](https://ftp.txdot.gov/pub/txdot/cst/cst-production-rates.pdf)). WSDOT gives per-8-hour rates by region, such as 12–36 in storm sewer at 800 LF/day West ([WSDOT](https://appstest.wsdot.wa.gov/publications/manuals/fulltext/M22-31/Appendix6.pdf)). These are not machine-hour rates, and they should not be stored as if they were. Their job is to bound the formula outputs. When a computed excavator spread implies 9,000 CY/day against a 2,030 median, the app should warn.

Outside earthwork, method beats numbers. Paver output is governed by plant and trucks, not by the paver. The Penn State/PennDOT booklet sizes a spread from mix yield and target tons/hr, and in its example 16 trucks feed 1,600 tons per shift ([Penn State Superpave](https://www.superpave.psu.edu/assets/docs/2025/Constructing-Quality-Asphalt-Pavements-Checklist-Booklet-2025-FINAL.pdf)). The Asphalt Institute limits rollers to 3 mph and sizes them by square yards placed ([Asphalt Institute](https://www.asphaltinstitute.org/faq/how-many-rollers-are-required/)). Concrete boom pumps place **60–100 CY/hr** in practice against a rated ~133 ([ForConstructionPros](https://www.forconstructionpros.com/concrete/article/21006661/placing-concrete-smarter)). Pile driving and drilled shafts depend so heavily on geology and diameter that the NCDOT (25 LF/day for a ≤60 in shaft in soil) and TxDOT (104 LF/day median) figures differ fourfold. Those activities should default to DOT rates with a visible "site-specific" flag. Units should stay native to the machine (LCY for blade, bucket and haul, CCY for compactors, SY or ft² for graders, acres for clearing) and convert to the takeoff's bid unit through the soil matrix. Most takeoff earthwork quantities are bank cut and compacted fill, so without that conversion every haul figure is wrong by 25–43%.

For duration-based equipment, hours come from the activity being served, not from the machine's own output. The rate-data publisher documents the 8/40/176 time basis and pro-rata overtime at 1/8, 1/40 and 1/176 of the rate (vendor documentation (link withheld, rule 9)). A lift on an MEP rough-in runs for that crew's duration. A dewatering pump runs 24 hours a day while the excavation is open, so its monthly overtime has to be priced. That fits the existing shared-resource rule directly: usage comes from crew hours on the host's labor component, or from a quantity formula.

## Mob/demob is a haul, and location needs three different dials

Per-move mob/demob is better supported by the evidence than a percentage. The only percentage norms found are DOT figures for whole-project mobilization. Caltrans notes contractors "normally bid their mobilization costs as 10 percent" ([Caltrans memo, 2006](https://sv08data.dot.ca.gov/contractcost/memo.pdf)). That number is a sanity check on total mobilization, not an equipment default. Haul cost depends on transport class and distance. 2026 trade guides put legal lowboy/RGN hauls at **$3.50–6.00 per loaded mile**, overweight loads at $6–12 and combined oversize/overweight at $8–15. Local minimums run $300–800 for a legal load and $800–2,500+ for an oversize one ([OTrucking RGN](https://otrucking.com/resources/guides/rgn-trailer-rates/); [OTrucking lowboy](https://otrucking.com/resources/guides/lowboy-trucking-rates/)). Texas publishes an exact permit schedule: $210 for 80,001–120,000 lb, rising to $435 above 200,000 lb ([TxDMV](https://www.txdmv.gov/motor-carriers/oversize-overweight-permits/general-single-trip)). The federal limits are 80,000 lb gross, 20,000 lb on a single axle and 34,000 lb on a tandem ([23 CFR 658.17](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/23/658.17)), with 8 ft 6 in (102 in) as the common width limit. A Cat 336-class excavator is about 133.5 in wide, so it is oversize on width alone ([RitchieSpecs](https://www.ritchiespecs.com/model/caterpillar-336d-l-hydraulic-excavator)). Rental yards charge flat delivery tiers instead. The Cat dealer charges **$224 under 25 mi and $364 for 25–60 mi**, then $8–18/mi by class ([Wheeler 2025](https://wheelercat.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/2025-Construction-Equipment-Rental-Rates.pdf)). Scissor lifts and booms up to about 60 ft run $75–350 each way, and 80 ft+ booms on a lowboy run $300–800+ ([ScaffoldExchange](https://scaffoldexchange.com/lifts-and-access/aerial-lift-rental-cost)). The OTrucking guides come from one publisher and contradict themselves in places, so the per-mile defaults are indicative. Diesel above $6/gal has also pushed 2026 haul rates up. DAT's September flatbed spot rate was $3.61/mi including a $1.01 fuel surcharge ([DAT](https://www.dat.com/company/news-events/news-releases/dat-truckload-rates-rise-in-september-as-diesel-tops-6-a-gallon)).

The practical design is to store transport weight, width and height on each row and let the app assign the transport class against the legal limits. The classes are drive/tow, hotshot, flatbed/step deck, legal lowboy, permitted lowboy, superload, and crane mob (multiple truckloads plus an assist crane). Mob and demob are stored separately because they can differ. The existing shared resource then counts mob once and spreads it by usage. Crawler cranes are a known blind spot: no public source gave assembly hours or counterweight truck counts.

On location, the evidence matches the split already decided and gives it numbers. In a cost-data publisher's 2025 City Cost Index for Illinois, the **Contractor Equipment index spans 89.9–105.1 across 18 cities while installation labor spans 99.3–144.2**, a 15-point against a 45-point spread. The two even move inversely: Chicago-area cities have the highest labor and the lowest equipment ([cost-data publisher's 2025 city cost index excerpt, secondary hosting](https://www.cliffsnotes.com/study-notes/28519784)). The rate-data publisher's regional multipliers for crawler excavators range from **82.2% (New Mexico) to 142.7% (Alaska North)**. They apply to ownership only, and the publisher names climate (shorter working seasons) as "the most important factor" (vendor documentation (link withheld, rule 9); vendor documentation (link withheld, rule 9)). Diesel ran from **$5.740 (Lower Atlantic) to $8.242 (California)** against a $6.276 US average ([EIA](https://www.eia.gov/petroleum/gasdiesel/)), a wider swing than equipment and on a different map. So the equipment factor should be narrow, defaulting to 1.00 and clamped near 0.85–1.15 in the contiguous US. It should apply to the bare rental rate and the owned ex-fuel rate only, never to fuel, the operator or the haul. The factor should sit with the Wage Calculator's location adjustment so one ZIP drives both, but as its own equipment factor beside the labor factor. Whether the calculator already stores an equipment factor is unverified and should be checked in the code before building. Two caveats belong in the UI. The national validation rests on one state of the cost-data publisher's index plus one class from the rate-data publisher. And rental prices follow local supply and demand, not cost: one 2001 United Rentals quote described regions "up 7 percent on the same day that another region is down 2.5 percent" ([RER](https://www.rermag.com/mag/article/20932580/all-over-the-place)). The location factor is therefore a default, and a local quote overrides it.

## A data model and picker that drop into the existing components

The model needs five reference tables and two user-side extensions. Values live in the library tables and are copied into components at pick time. Every row carries its sources, date and confidence grade, so the picker can show where a number came from.

| Entity | Key fields | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| `equipment_category` → `equipment_type` | name, sort, parent | About 23 top-level categories reconciled from FEMA's code groups and Caltrans' class codes: Earthmoving, Hauling, Compaction, Trenching & Boring, Shoring, Foundations, Cranes, Material Handling, Aerial Work Platforms, Concrete, Paving, Clearing, Demolition, Air & Power, Pumps, Sewer & Vac, Trucks, Trailers, Traffic, Site Services, Erosion Control, optional Marine and Snow |
| `equipment_class` (the catalog row) | type_id, size_metric (op_weight_lb, net_hp, bucket_yd3, roc_lb, payload_t, platform_ft, cfm, kw…), size_min/max, subtype flags (tail swing, track/wheel, narrow/wide, RT/slab, electric/engine, jib), example models, transport weight/width/height, transport_class (auto-derived, overridable), pricing_mode (production / duration / operated_service), fuel_type | One row per type × size band; FEMA "up to HP" rows and dealer weight classes define the bands |
| `equipment_rate` (versioned) | class_id, effective_date, rent_day/week/4wk (median), rent_min/max, source_count, rental_hour_basis (8/40/160), owned_rate_all_in, owned_includes_fuel, owned_rate_ex_fuel (derived), owned_basis (FEMA ceiling / proxy / none), standby_factor, ot_factor, operated_hourly + min_hours (operated_service only), confidence (A/B/C) | Confidence derives from source count and dates; a new version never alters existing estimates |
| `equipment_fuel` | class_id, gph_low/med/high, basis (Cat telematics / HP × 0.0486 × load factor) | Load level maps to Cat's 50/30/10% idle definitions |
| `equipment_activity` | class_id, activity (cut & load, mass haul, dozer push/spread, strip, fine grade, compact, trench, pave, mill, pump, drive pile…), native_unit (LCY/hr, CCY/hr, SY/hr, LF/hr, AC/hr), method (formula id or fixed rate), default inputs, ideal_rate, efficiency default 0.83, dot_daily_low/typ/high, bid_unit conversion (soil matrix) | Many activities per machine; activity names should be confirmed against Umer's own vocabulary before seeding |
| `workspace_equipment` (company list) | class_id or custom, overridden rates, owned internal rate, favourite, crew link | The "Lists" library; snapshot-on-add, refreshable |
| Project settings | fuel price by fuel type (default from regional EIA, user-entered wins), rental surcharge % (waiver + environmental), haul origin miles | Equipment location factor stored beside the Wage Calculator's labor factor (check whether one exists already) |

The equipment component gains a small set of fields: `class_id`, `rate_basis` (rental or owned), `billing_basis` (auto-cheapest, hour, day, week, 4-week), `hours_source` (crew hours from the item's labor component, activity production, quantity formula, or manual), `fuel_load` (low/med/high), `activity_id` (production classes only), the snapshot of rates at pick time, and the existing link to a shared division resource. The cost resolves as follows. Equipment cost = bare rate (owned ex-fuel × hours, or rental by the cheapest period combination covering the duration) × equipment location factor, plus gal/hr × hours × project fuel price, plus rental surcharge %. Mob/demob sits on the shared resource and is spread by usage. The operator is never on the equipment component. When the class has a linked operator craft, the picker offers to add it to the item's labor crew at the project's saved wage set. This follows product A's operator link, but routes the operator into labor rather than into the equipment rate.

The picker should open on search plus category → type → size facets. For each class it shows the defining spec range and example models. The rate panel shows median rental day/week/4-week with the range, owned $/hr ex-fuel labeled as a ceiling or proxy, fuel $/hr at the project's price, the location factor in use, the default transport class with its mob and demob, and a confidence badge. For production classes, a second step lists that machine's activities with native unit, ideal and adjusted rate, and the DOT daily band. Choosing one sets `hours_source = activity` and offers to push the production rate into the crew, so crew hours and equipment hours stay one number, as in product A. Otherwise the equipment follows the crew's hours. For duration classes, the picker skips the activity step and defaults to the host's crew hours or a quantity formula. Operated-service classes (cranes, pumps, trucking) show the operated hourly rate and minimum, and they suppress the operator prompt. Picking from the national library adds the row to the company list automatically, so favourites and company overrides build up over time. An assembly stores the class name and usage formula, and applying it links to the matching project resource, as the existing assembly rule already specifies.

Phases 1–3 deliver a usable library without any production data. Phase 4 is where IntelCost can lead the takeoff-first tools, because none of them ties a takeoff quantity to machine hours. Phase 5 is mostly a licensing decision, not an engineering one.

## Conclusion

The public data is unevenly strong. Rental rates for common machines are well supported, owned rates are serviceable ceilings, location behaviour is well evidenced, and machine-specific production curves are largely missing. That should shape the product. The library will win on structure first: size-class rows, cheapest-period billing, pooled machines with visible idle time, and mob/demob as hauls. Its data quality improves afterward through company overrides and, if the economics work, licensed sources. Three findings change the design itself, beyond confirming it. The FEMA-versus-rental comparison shows the owned column cannot be presented as a neutral "national average": it needs a ceiling label and an easy company override. At October 2026 diesel prices, fuel is roughly a fifth of a dozer's hourly cost, so the fuel price input belongs in project setup, not buried in settings. And the soil conversion matrix is what makes the takeoff-to-equipment-hours link correct, because a bank-versus-loose error is larger than any rate error in the library.

The open questions are narrow and answerable: a Texas/California/Florida rental sheet, a 2026 lift rate list, the verified FEMA rows, the Cat dozer and excavator curves, and confirmation from Umer of the activity names and the operated-service exception for cranes, pumps and trucking.
